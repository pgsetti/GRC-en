Before we create our first policy, I'd like to discuss the G (Governance) in a little more detail.
The reason is that, without this first step, any GRC project will die at conception. If your executive board (and if you're a director, this applies to you too) doesn't buy into the idea, unfortunately no risk or compliance action will be carried out the right way. Everything starts at the G.

## But why does this matter?
In practice, this means that everything starts with governance and you need very robust backing to move forward with the project. We call this the Sponsor. In other words, someone with high-level decision-making power, like directors, CEOs, CISOs, needs to sponsor the GRC process as a whole, including but not limited to the financial side and the resources (such as employees' time).
Whether you're a sponsor or a technical person, it doesn't matter: this post will bring important insights for both sides. Both are fundamental to the process, one practically doesn't exist without the other, and both sides acting correctly is the only way to guarantee that everything will work. There are no obstacles here, only allies with the same objective.
I want you to understand this clearly, because the vast majority of GRC processes come into existence in the wrong format. They only show up REACTIVELY, when an audit comes up, when a client requests a specific compliance, or, in the worst case, a cyber incident (which can cause the other two).
The problem with the reactive process is that it works like a band-aid chasing after the next "injury". GRC stops thinking as a complete, structured process and starts chasing the next fix and the next fix… If you've had any contact with programming, this is very common, you ship the next update after the next update, but you never think about the code as a whole. In the end, there's only one outcome: you're going to break.
Usually because your process has become completely unmaintainable, the documentation starts to drown you and it becomes impossible to understand what you have, what you don't have and what's missing. It also makes presenting data much harder. Ask yourself the following:

> "If I had to deliver a complete report of everything I have, how it was implemented, the evidence for it, which are the originating controls and what my compliance percentage is against market frameworks, could I do that in less than 1 day?"
A well-structured project never suffers from a lack of data (usually it's the opposite, actually), since you've thought about every point and about how they talk to each other.
The first and main symptoms of a flawed process are:
- **Lack of authority:** GRC loses the authority to define rules and processes for the other areas. So your best-practice definitions don't get implemented, and they simply ignore them.
- **Business vision:** A limited GRC (and here I'm referring mainly to the cybersecurity one) usually has heavy involvement from the IT area. Of course the next step is evolving into a complete security area, but that isn't the case most of the time. The detail here is that if IT (or whoever is responsible for GRC) doesn't have a view of the business as a whole, of all the processes, areas and data, it's impossible to define what's critical, what has to be improved and what needs to change to adapt to compliance.
- **The mess:** This one seems simple, but often we don't even notice we're entering a black hole of meaningless policies and processes. You'll definitely notice that nobody knows where the documents live, where the evidence is and what we need in order to fulfill each item.

But there's one thing I want to make very clear. None of this is fatal! In fact, noticing these problems is a good thing, since you can change it. By changing the GRC process it's possible to fix any mistake.
The catch is that we're back to the beginning of this text… does your board see it that way too?
If the answer is "YES", that's the best of all worlds, of course, you already have the awareness and the authorization to change and improve this process.
But if the answer is "NO", there's no point trying to change everything, you'll be blocked. So it's time to prepare a good executive presentation and explain in detail why this change is so important.
The part that gets me most excited about all of this is that we're increasingly seeing companies as a whole realize the validity of it and take up the fight so that processes become robust and very well defined. That's great news for cybersecurity.

## What is this, anyway?
The sponsor's role is to be closely involved, but not as the "owner" of the GRC process, that still falls to the more technical team (not necessarily IT). But they'll act at several levels. Let's understand the main ones:
- **Active and Visible Support:** The sponsor must communicate to the whole company that "this is a priority".
- **Provision of Resources:** Guarantee the budget and, more importantly, the time of people from different areas to take part in the process.
- **Decision-Making:** Being the final "referee". When the sales area says a security control gets in the way of closing a deal, it's the sponsor who makes the final call, based on the company's strategy.

And notice that the "business" vision we mentioned earlier is heavily supported by the sponsor, and without him the policy will die at the first resistance from a department. And understand that this resistance is super common, security has always been an enemy of convenience, unfortunately.
Imagine that locking your house every day is annoying. It's much easier to just not lock anything and come and go whenever you need to, but nobody does that, we know the security implications. Unfortunately, that mentality is sometimes ignored when it comes to cybersecurity.
Nobody likes rotating passwords every 45 days, that's a fact, I don't like it either. But the key point here isn't about what to do or not do, it's about explaining the reason for it. Your parents probably explained to you why you lock the doors when you leave early in the morning, we need to do the same thing for every GRC item. It's not about trying to convince people that it isn't an inconvenience (because it is), it's about explaining why that inconvenience is fundamental and necessary for everyone.
But there's a very important item that only governance can define, and that we haven't talked about before, something we call Risk Appetite, and the importance of this item is enormous even before starting any GRC process. Risk Appetite is a guideline and often generates a document called a RAS (Risk Appetite Statement).

Let's say you want to fix the largest possible number of risks inside the organization, that can be almost impossible. The chance of someone suffering a social engineering attack may be low, but it's never zero. The only way to bring a risk to zero is to eliminate it completely.
Want to eliminate the risk of social engineering? Don't have any employees, not even yourself.
That makes no sense at all, of course we're never going to chase zero, the idea is to get as close to it as possible. But what defines that we're "close enough" to accept the risk? Risk Appetite!
Creating this guideline is exactly for that: defining "close enough". That way, we know that where the risk sits at the present moment is as far as we're going to act.

Picture the following: if you own a company or work at a startup focused on technological innovation, your risk appetite is probably going to be higher than that of a healthcare institution. The reason lies in each company's model, one aims to adopt new technologies and improve as fast as possible, the other focuses on data security and stability (continuity).
Notice that risk appetite can change drastically between the most conservative possible (without aiming for zeros, of course, that's impossible) and the most open possible, where risks are barely treated at all.
To be honest, you'll usually want to sit in the middle of the spectrum, not too much and not too little, but even so you'll shift which side you lean toward according to your market, product, clients and business objectives.

## How does this work in practice?
As I mentioned earlier, the process starts with the G (governance). Before starting anything, it's 100% necessary that the board, C-Levels and all of senior management are aligned and have an idea of what this can generate. But practice goes beyond an "ok, go ahead" meeting.
For GRC to work and for you not to become "the annoying security guy" nobody listens to, you need to establish the rules of the game. And the golden rule here is to define who's who.
Many projects fail because the IT/Security team thinks it owns all of the company's risks. That's a brutal mistake.

Let's separate the roles:
- **The Sponsor:** The one who provides the authority and the resources (money and time). Usually the CEO or the Board.
- **The Executor (GRC Team):** Their role is to identify the hole, measure how deep it is, suggest the ladder to get out of there and monitor whether the ladder is steady.
- **The Risk Owner:** The risk owner is not IT. The owner of the risk of an HR data leak… is the HR director. The owner of the risk of fraud in finance… is the CFO, and so on.

Why does this distinction save your life (and the process)?
Imagine you've identified a critical risk in the sales system. You suggest implementing Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).

The Sales director says:
> No! That's going to slow the process down and we're going to lose sales. I won't accept it.
Without Governance, you'd start a fight until one of the sides gave up.

With Governance, you document the risk, present the consequences and, if the sales director (the risk owner) decides not to implement it and to accept the risk (based on that risk appetite we defined earlier), he signs off on the responsibility.
That makes him a fundamental "part" of the problem.

If there's an incident tomorrow, the decision was a business one, backed by governance, and not an "IT failure".
That takes it off the GRC team and puts the responsibility where it should be: with the business leadership.

The secret here is to give the greatest possible visibility and to bring expertise to help each area with the process, not to solve the problem for them.
